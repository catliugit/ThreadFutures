Every unit a second life, and the proof.

Thread Futures is the trusted second-life desk for the stock nobody else will take: returns, damaged, broken size runs, old seasons and logo-heavy lines from brands selling into the EU. We give every unit a second life through vetted buyers and EU charities we fund to say yes, and hand the brand a file proving where all of it went.

Since 19 July 2026, large brands can't destroy unsold clothes and shoes in the EU, and that includes shredding them for recycling. The easy stock already sells: off-price chains buy it directly. The hard part is the tail. Jobbers take it cheaply and can't say where it ends up; charities refuse 15–20% of what's offered because they can't pay to move or store it. We grade it, sell it to contracted buyers who report where it goes, donate the rest to EU charities with the logistics paid, recycle only as a last resort, and hand the brand a destination file for every batch.

Why we changed course (12 Nov 2026): the software we'd built (stall-risk model, regulation search, order agent) can largely be reproduced by a brand analyst with an AI assistant, and clean stock doesn't need us. So we sell a recurring outcome and a file, not software and not introductions.

The short version: Phenix for fashion, under the EU ban. Phenix has done this for food and household goods in France since 2014. It routes retailers' unsold stock to about 3,000 charities, handles the paperwork, earns most of its money from monthly subscriptions, and has been EBITDA-positive since 2023 on roughly €20m revenue. It doesn't take clothing worn next to the skin and has no fashion brands. That's the gap we want to fill. We'll start with stock already sitting in EU warehouses, so nothing has to cross the UK–EU border.

Business model · hypotheses to test in pilots

Brands pay a subscription. Buyers pay for goods.

Subscription first, commission as upside. The hardest stock recovers the least money, so a fee that depends only on sale value doesn't work: Otrium dropped brands whose cheap items couldn't cover costs on commission, while Phenix earns about 80% of its revenue from subscriptions. We don't buy stock. Logistics, repair, sorting and recycling are done by partners and passed through at cost. All prices below are illustrative and will be tested in pilots.

Defensibility

Why they won't cut us out.

One-off introductions leak: past a certain level of trust, parties go direct. So we sell a recurring outcome and a file. Contracted buyers, physical grading, custody records made at each handover and a funded EU charity network are things a chatbot can't create after the fact.

Competition

Many players own one piece of the tail.

We're independent and built for fashion. We work with whatever warehouse a brand already uses, place a batch across several routes (sale, donation, and recycling only as a last resort), grade by size, condition and fibre, cover more than one EU country, and produce rows ready for the EU disclosure table. A donation-only record already exists for free in France, so the multi-route file is the point. The closest competitor is PreZero ReValue, a waste company that takes ownership of stock and resells it abroad with proof of resale. The big fashion warehouses are building their own versions too. Our first customers: premium and mid-market brands led by clothing and footwear, which the ban covers (handbags appear to fall outside it).

Main risks: brands may find their jobber, charity partner and a spreadsheet good enough (our explicit kill signal); the hardest stock recovers the least money (hence subscription-first pricing); PreZero, Bleckmann or Phenix could move faster into fashion (we stay independent and partner with mid-size warehouses); I don't come from fashion operations, and every company like this started with an insider (so an adviser and a commercial co-founder come first); shipping UK stock into the EU is slow and costly since Brexit (so we start with stock already in EU warehouses); whether a brand is liable if a buyer later destroys goods is not settled (lawyer check, and no-destruction clauses in every sale contract); enforcement varies by country (Germany's fines start 1 Nov 2026); parties going direct (anonymity, split batches, annual programmes); a solo founder running an operational business (partners do logistics). We won't sell our own label or logo for brands to use: since September 2026 EU law bans sustainability labels that aren't backed by an independent certification scheme, so we sell evidence a brand's lawyers and auditors can rely on, which an accredited body could audit later. This is general information, not legal advice.
