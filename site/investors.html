For investors and programmes

We find new homes for the clothes fashion brands can't sell.

Since July 2026, large fashion brands selling into the EU, including UK brands, can't destroy unsold clothes. Thread Futures is a B2B resale service for fashion brands: we act as their sales team for stock they can't sell themselves, selling it to off-price retailers, resale platforms and stock buyers, and give them the money from each sale plus a report of where every item went. Our pricing hypothesis, to be tested in pilots: a free first pilot, then a season subscription plus a share of what we recover.

The problem: Brands now need a legal home for every unsold item. Clean, current stock already has buyers. The problem is the stock liquidators won't take: returns, damaged items, odd sizes and old seasons. Charities refuse 15–20% of the donations they're offered because they can't fund transport, destroying it is no longer allowed, and recycling counts as destruction unless an exception is documented. For this stock, the usual alternative today is no option at all: it sits in storage at a cost, with no legal route out. (Charity refusals: Agence du Don en Nature, France. Liquidators typically pay ~5–20% of retail for stock they do take: rough industry estimate.)

Like Phenix (about €20m revenue in France, for food and household goods), built for fashion.

We make the better option the easy one, then use what we learn to help brands make less.

In brief

The service, the buyer and the route in.

What Thread Futures does, who pays, how we reach them, and why a service rather than software. Beliefs below are hypotheses to test in interviews, not findings.

Go-to-market

3PLs first, brands buy.

We interview and partner with mid-size fashion warehouses (3PLs) first. They see the unsellable stock every month and introduce us to the brands they work for. The brand, through its operations or merchandising lead, is the paying customer. Direct brand outreach runs in parallel, as a second route.

How it works

The brand sends us a list of what it can't sell: returns, damaged items, old seasons. A partner warehouse checks every item for condition and size. We sell what we can to off-price stores, resale sites and stock buyers, with the brand's name hidden; what doesn't sell goes to charities that need it, with the transport paid; damaged items go to a licensed recycler. The brand gets its money, minus our share, and a report of where every item went. We coordinate and keep the record; partners do the physical work.

Why we believe brands will use it (to test)

Money back on stock that today has no route at all, where the alternative is storage cost and no legal way out, and checking each item lets it go to the buyer who pays most; avoiding fines of up to €100,000 per breach in Germany, with evidence kept for five years and produced within 30 days; brand protection, through anonymous lots, resale limits and de-labelling where needed, so the label does not end up on the grey market; staff time and storage saved; and, over time, insight into what didn't sell, so brands make less.

How we're different

Otrium and B-Stock sell excess stock that is easy to sell, Trove runs branded resale, Phenix routes unsold goods to charities outside fashion, and PreZero ReValue takes ownership of stock and exports it. Thread Futures handles the leftover stock they won't take, through every route from resale to donation to recycling; the brand keeps ownership of the stock until it is sold, and we keep one record across all of it.

Why a service, not software

We sell an outcome and an evidence record, not software. Clean stock already sells, and analysing a stock file is something a brand analyst with an AI assistant can largely do. What can't be created after the fact is the physical placement and the records made at each handover, so that is what we handle.

Business model · hypotheses to test in pilots

A free first pilot, then a subscription.

The first pilot batch is free, so a brand can judge the service on real numbers; in return we get a case study. After that, a season subscription plus a share of what we recover. The hardest stock recovers the least money, so a fee that depends only on sale value doesn't work: Otrium dropped brands whose cheap items couldn't cover costs on commission, while Phenix earns about 80% of its revenue from subscriptions. The brand keeps ownership of the stock until it is sold. Logistics, repair, sorting and recycling are done by partners and passed through at cost. This is a hypothesis to be tested in pilots. How we think about the economics: for the stock we target (returns, odd sizes, damaged items, old seasons), the usual alternative is no option at all: storage cost and, since the ban, no legal route out, so the comparison that matters is against nothing. Where we don't compete: clean, current stock that liquidators and off-price stores already buy. Where a liquidator would take a batch, our service only makes sense if checking each item and selling it to the right buyer recovers clearly more than the liquidator pays. That recovery rate is the key test in our interviews and pilots. Unit economics and early scenarios are in our deck, available on request.

Tax, to check with an adviser: where a brand has an EU entity, the fee and transport may count toward the value of a donation (in France, 60% of that value comes back as a tax reduction; Phenix's terms work this way). UK tax relief only applies to gifts to UK charities. Charities: we may tier this. Curated, sellable lots could carry a small handling fee, as the French Agence du Don en Nature charges; hard-to-place stock moves on brand-funded logistics. We'll test both in charity interviews.

Why us

Why brands and buyers won't go around us.

One-off introductions leak: past a certain level of trust, parties go direct. So we sell a recurring outcome and the record that goes with it. Contracted buyers, physical grading, custody records made at each handover and a funded EU charity network are things a chatbot can't create after the fact.

AI & data advantage

A service business that gets smarter with every batch.

People handle trust, contracts and the physical work. AI handles the work that grows with volume: reading stock lists, matching lots to buyers and drafting the records. Each batch also adds outcome data that no single brand holds. The labels below say honestly what exists today.

The moat

The data and the network, not the model. Anyone can use the same AI models. What compounds is what only running the service produces:

  • Outcome data across brands: what didn't sell, why, where it went and at what price.
  • A network of vetted buyers and charities, with their acceptance rules and prices.
  • The evidence trail made at each handover, which can't be rebuilt after the fact.
Why can't ChatGPT do this?

It can read a stock file. It can't find a buyer for 3,000 pairs of size 44–46 trainers this week, arrange the collection, or prove three donation offers were made. That knowledge sits with buyers and charities, and the evidence exists only if someone runs the process. We use the same models; what we add is the network, the handovers and the data they create.

Guardrails

A person reviews anything a brand signs off. We don't claim automation that doesn't exist: each step is marked built, in pilots or later. Brand data is handled under contract.

Competition

Many players own one piece of the problem.

We're independent and built for fashion. We work with whatever warehouse a brand already uses, sell a batch across several routes (sale first, donation next, and recycling only as a last resort), check each item for size, condition and fibre, cover more than one EU country, and produce rows ready for the EU disclosure table. A donation-only record already exists for free in France, so one record across every route is the point. The closest competitor is PreZero ReValue, a waste company that takes ownership of stock and resells it abroad with proof of resale. The big fashion warehouses are building their own versions too. Our first customers: premium and mid-market brands led by clothing and footwear, which the ban covers (handbags appear to fall outside it).
